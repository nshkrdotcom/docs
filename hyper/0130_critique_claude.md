Reading all four documents together, here are the remaining gaps that none of the twenty-six responses have addressed.

**The system has no model of reading as a primary activity.** All the workflows are oriented toward people who know what they're looking for or who are responding to a finding. But a large portion of architecture work is careful, slow reading: an engineer sits with a module for an hour, follows threads, builds a mental model, notices something odd, revisits it. The exploration session captures attention trails, but it doesn't support the phenomenology of sustained reading — the ability to annotate, pause and return, hold multiple threads open, compare two things side by side, mark something as "I don't understand this yet," or signal that you've read and provisionally accepted something. Without this, the system treats every interaction as either a query or a navigation step, missing the slower interpretive work that precedes all of those.

**There's no model of partial understanding as a valid persistent state.** Related to the above: the design treats understanding as something you either have or are building toward. But "I partially understand this and have accepted the uncertainty for now" is a durable, legitimate cognitive state that should be storable. An engineer might read a module, understand 70% of it, decide the other 30% is low-risk for current purposes, and mark it as good enough for the task at hand. That verdict should persist, be revisitable, and decay appropriately as the code changes. Currently the system would represent this as a collection of unknown claims with no wrapper indicating that the ambiguity was seen and accepted.

**The system has no theory of what makes an explanation good for a specific person.** The trust UX covers how to present uncertainty, evidence, and belief states. The compression engine covers how to minimize cognitive load. But there's no model of the reader. A junior engineer looking at their first Elixir codebase needs different explanations than a senior architect doing due diligence on an acquisition. The system can be told an audience exists via query parameters, but it has no persistent model of individual understanding, background, or prior exposure. This means explanations can't adapt to what a person already knows, can't skip things they've already internalized, and can't build on their personal vocabulary and mental model. Every interaction starts from scratch.

**The system models architecture primarily as structure and constraint, but not as narrative.** Architecture has a story: this thing was built this way because of that constraint, which came from that business situation, which changed when this other thing happened. The design-to-code hypergraph captures the pieces of this, but not the narrative arc. Real architectural understanding requires being able to tell the story of why the system looks the way it does, in a form that makes the current state feel intelligible rather than arbitrary. The system can answer "what requirement led to this function" but can't answer "tell me the story of how the billing system got into its current shape and why it makes sense given the history." That narrative capability is distinct from trace queries and projections.

**There's no model of silence and absence as intentional signals.** When a well-maintained codebase has no ADR for a decision, that might mean the decision was never made, or it might mean it was so obvious it didn't need documentation. When there are no tests for a module, that might be a gap or it might be intentional for a prototype. The system treats absence as either unknown or missing, but it doesn't model intentional silence: the decision to not document something, the conscious choice to leave a boundary informal, the deliberate acceptance of a known gap. This matters because the system will otherwise generate noise treating intentional informality as missing architecture.

**The system has no model of the cost of formalization itself.** The entire design pushes toward making architecture more explicit: commitments, ADRs, governance, exceptions, formal boundaries. But formalization has costs. It creates maintenance burden, it constrains future options, it can calcify prematurely, it takes time to produce and time to read. For a small team or a rapidly evolving product, heavy formalization is worse than lighter convention. The system should have a theory of when formalization adds value and when it adds overhead, and it should be able to operate at different formalization levels without constantly suggesting that the right answer is always more explicit architecture. This is partly about configuration, but it's more fundamentally about the system's epistemic stance toward informality.

**Cross-cutting concerns that span the entire abstraction stack are unmodeled as first-class objects.** Security, performance, observability, and internationalization appear in the dimensional coordinate system, but they aren't modeled as architecture concerns that have their own coherent structure across all abstraction levels simultaneously. A security concern isn't just a tag on individual functions — it has its own requirements, its own design decisions, its own code patterns, its own test surfaces, its own runtime evidence, and its own governance. The system can filter by security concern but can't navigate "the security architecture" as a coherent object that exists alongside and through the domain architecture. This means security reviewers, for example, have no home in the system — they can filter findings by security tag but can't see the security architecture as a unified navigable space.

**The system has no model of the relationship between architecture and time-to-market.** The debt economics layer handles "which problems are worth fixing" under capacity constraints. But there's a prior question: what is the architecture doing to development velocity right now, and how would different architectural choices change it? This isn't about debt cleanup — it's about the live cost of the current architecture on the team's ability to ship. High coupling makes changes harder. Missing boundaries make reasoning harder. Unclear ownership makes coordination harder. These costs show up in PR review times, in how long it takes to understand a change's impact, in how many people need to be involved in decisions. The system should be able to estimate and monitor these velocity costs, not just detect technical debt.

**The design assumes relatively stable team composition.** The organizational time layer handles team lineage events, but the practical reality of rotating engineers, contractors, onboarding and offboarding, context loss during leaves, and knowledge concentration in key people isn't modeled at the level of daily operational concerns. Who on the current team actually understands this module well enough to review a change to it? Who has the context to approve this exception? Who should be in the room for this architecture decision? The system has ownership and governance bodies, but not a model of individual human expertise and availability as an architectural resource that needs management.

The thread connecting most of these is that the design treats architecture as primarily an objective artifact — something that exists in code and documents and can be represented, queried, and enforced. The gaps cluster around the subjective, temporal, and social dimensions: how individuals understand, how understanding accumulates and decays, how narrative and meaning are constructed, how formalization itself has costs, how attention and expertise are scarce and unevenly distributed. The system is excellent at the artifact layer and increasingly good at the process layer, but the human cognitive and social layer remains mostly a set of inputs and outputs rather than a modeled domain in its own right.
