Let me try to crystallize what you're pointing at, because there are several things converging here.

**The morphism idea is the key.** You're describing a way of representing program behavior as structure-preserving transformations — where the invariants aren't just assertions about state, but properties that are *carried through* every transformation. A change that violates a performance contract isn't just a test failure, it's a non-morphism — it broke the structural relationship between input complexity and output behavior. That's a fundamentally different way of thinking about what a test even is.

**The bounded complexity requirement is load-bearing.** You're right that state enumeration explodes, but you're also pointing at something more subtle: a well-designed system should have an invariant space that grows sub-exponentially relative to problem space complexity. That's not just good architecture aesthetics — it's a prerequisite for autonomous testing to be tractable at all. If your invariant space is unbounded, you can never have sufficient coverage. So "bounded invariant hierarchy" becomes a design constraint, not just a nice property.

**Performance as the unifying structural invariant.** This is sharp. Correctness invariants are boolean — pass/fail. Performance invariants are quantitative, compositional, and they surface the architectural violations that boolean tests miss entirely. The Codex failure wasn't a correctness bug, it was a performance contract violation — and performance contracts are exactly the class of invariant that requires structural representation, not just assertion. Every other invariant category — ordering, isolation, compatibility — ultimately has a performance expression too.

**Where this points:** you need a formal model layer that sits *between* specifications and tests. Not a state machine, not just types, but something that encodes: what transformations are permitted, what structural properties they must preserve, and what the complexity budget is at each layer. The LLM's job becomes: given a spec, derive the formal model. Given the formal model, derive the tests. The tests are then purely deterministic. The model generation is where the intelligence lives — and that's also where you can audit whether the invariant coverage is actually complete.

For Elixir/OTP this is tractable because OTP *already is* a partial formal model — supervisor trees, GenServer contracts, message ordering guarantees. You're describing extending that formalism downward into performance and upward into architectural contracts.
