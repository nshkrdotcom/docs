# Industry Evaluation and Reader Criteria: An Exhaustive Product Survey

## Purpose

This document translates professional screenplay-reading and development criteria into capabilities for a screenplay-intelligence system. It is deliberately broader than any single craft methodology. The goal is not to teach the system to imitate one guru. The goal is to understand **what competent readers repeatedly attend to**, which observations can be responsibly modeled, which judgments remain holistic, and how those distinctions should shape Fount.

## 1. Three different activities are often confused

A screenplay may be read for at least three materially different purposes.

### 1.1 Selection / competition reading

The reader asks whether a script distinguishes itself strongly enough to advance. This emphasizes overall reaction, originality, voice, emotional connection, character vividness, craft, and the desire to continue reading.

The Academy Nicholl rubric is unusually useful because it makes these dimensions explicit without requiring compliance with a named structure system. Its categories include Story, Voice, Characters, Craft, and Meaning and Magic. The questions cover freshness of premise, beginning/middle/end coherence, feeling taken on a journey, emotional connection, distinctive voice, vivid characters, character change, dialogue/tone consistency, suspense/tension/drama/comedy/conflict, conflict that propels the story, character actions that move the story, whether those actions fit the people, reader desire to continue, thought-provoking themes, and an ineffable quality that elevates the script.

**System implication:** selection rubrics are a coverage map, not a numeric ontology. They tell Fount what dimensions it must not ignore. They do not justify compressing those dimensions into a universal score.

### 1.2 Studio/agency coverage

Coverage commonly considers concept/premise, story/plot, character, dialogue, structure, pacing, originality, tone, theme, and sometimes commercial/casting/production concerns. Coverage is an organizational decision aid. It mixes craft reaction with business context.

**System implication:** Fount should distinguish **craft intelligence** from **acquisition/market intelligence**. Marketability, budget, casting, slate fit, and commercial positioning should not contaminate dramatic diagnoses unless an explicit future product surface adds them.

### 1.3 Development notes

Development is not primarily about ranking a draft. It is about identifying where the intended movie is not yet landing, understanding why, and exploring revision strategies without destroying what already works.

**System implication:** this is Fount's natural center of gravity. The system should be strongest at evidence, comparison, hypotheses, alternatives, and revision consequences rather than pass/consider/recommend grading.

## 2. What institutional criteria consistently reveal

Across the Academy rubric, Sundance development materials, professional coverage practice, and experienced screenwriter discussions, several recurring dimensions emerge.

### 2.1 Story journey and forward movement

Readers care not only that events occur, but that the movie feels like it is **going somewhere**.

Observable components include:

- established goals and changing goals;
- causes and consequences;
- decisions that alter later possibilities;
- new constraints and losses;
- escalating or transforming problems;
- sequence objectives and sequence outcomes;
- unanswered dramatic questions;
- reversals and discoveries;
- movement toward or away from a desired state.

Fount should model forward movement as a temporal property. A locally functional scene can still contribute little new movement if it repeats a goal, reiterates known information, restores the same relationship state, and creates no new consequence.

### 2.2 Emotional connection

Professional readers repeatedly refer to caring about characters, wanting to know what happens to them, emotional connection, catharsis, and feeling something from the read.

This is not reducible to “likability.” Relevant system-visible contributors include:

- clear stakes or vulnerability;
- access to a character's desire or dilemma;
- costly choices;
- anticipation of gain/loss;
- earned reversals;
- relationship attachment;
- moral or psychological conflict;
- congruence between event and reaction;
- cumulative investment in unresolved goals.

Fount must treat emotional engagement as a **reader-state hypothesis**, not a fact embedded in a line of dialogue.

### 2.3 Distinctive voice

“Voice” operates at multiple scales:

- writer voice: selection, tone, rhythm, worldview, image patterns, comic/dramatic sensibility;
- character voice: lexical, syntactic, rhetorical, strategic, and worldview distinctions;
- film voice: the distinctive combination of premise, structure, tone, world, and dramatic choices.

The current voice-classification idea covers only part of this. A future system should preserve the distinction between “can I identify which character said this?” and “does this script feel authored by a singular intelligence?” The latter should remain interpretive and comparative.

### 2.4 Vivid, specific characters

Recurring reader concerns include specificity, desire, motivation, contradictions, behavior, agency, relationships, and change.

System dimensions:

- goal/want/wish distinction;
- external objective versus internal need/desire;
- tactic and tactic change;
- values and commitments;
- knowledge/belief state;
- recurring defense or behavioral pattern;
- relationship-specific behavior;
- decision ownership;
- consequences caused by the character;
- adaptation after consequences;
- arc or deliberate refusal/inability to change.

A character need not transform for a film to work. Therefore “arc” must support several forms: transformation, revelation, steadfast testing, corruption, disillusionment, cyclical failure, tragic inability to change, ensemble divergence, or deliberately minimal change.

### 2.5 Conflict, pressure, and stakes

Conflict should not be defined as arguing. Professional craft discussion repeatedly returns to incompatible wants, obstacles, choices, pressure, risk, and consequences.

Fount should represent:

- what each participant wants **in this scene**;
- incompatibility or resource competition;
- external obstacle versus interpersonal opposition versus internal conflict;
- consequences of failure;
- immediacy/urgency;
- leverage and power;
- what decision becomes necessary;
- what changes if the character succeeds or fails.

### 2.6 Causal character action

The Academy rubric explicitly asks whether main-character actions move the story and whether those actions fit who the characters are. Scriptnotes similarly emphasizes tracking what a character wants and the actions taken toward it.

This suggests two separate machine questions:

1. **causal agency:** did this character's choice materially create the next state/event?
2. **character coherence:** is that choice supported by established desire, knowledge, belief, pressure, relationship, or prior behavior?

Neither should become “protagonists must cause every event.” Stories may intentionally subject characters to forces beyond their control. The useful question is whether the dramatic pattern matches the intended movie.

### 2.7 Dialogue and interaction

Industry evaluation typically treats dialogue as its own category, but dialogue quality is not just elegant line writing.

Fount should model:

- response/evasion relation to the preceding turn;
- tactic and tactic shift;
- directness versus subtext;
- exposition burden;
- redundancy/repetition;
- status/leverage bids;
- concealment and disclosure;
- interruptions/withholding;
- voice distinction;
- rhythm and turn length;
- relationship-specific vocabulary;
- whether dialogue changes knowledge, objective, pressure, or relationship state.

The strongest abstraction is **interaction**, not isolated lines.

### 2.8 Structure and pacing

Professional readers care about structure and pacing even when they disagree about formula.

Fount should therefore model structural phenomena without requiring a fixed paradigm:

- scene and sequence boundaries;
- density of consequential change;
- unresolved-question lifetimes;
- repetition intervals;
- goal persistence;
- escalation and release;
- information rate;
- relationship movement rate;
- action/dialogue density;
- entry before conflict and exit after consequence;
- concentration of setup/payoff activity;
- distribution of turning events;
- long spans in which no tracked dramatic state changes.

This supports many structural schools without hard-coding one.

### 2.9 Originality and freshness

Nicholl and Sundance both emphasize fresh stories/voices. This is difficult to evaluate responsibly from a screenplay alone because originality is relative to a reader's experience and the surrounding culture.

Fount may safely support:

- self-similarity within a draft;
- repetition/cliché candidates against optional external corpora;
- unusual combinations of genre/world/character premise;
- writer-specified comps and deliberate departures;
- internal pattern novelty;
- alternative strategy contrast.

It should not declare “originality = 83%.”

### 2.10 Theme, meaning, and purpose

Institutional readers care whether the story feels “about something,” but theme should not be reduced to a slogan.

A useful system can trace:

- repeated value conflicts;
- decisions that instantiate those conflicts;
- consequences that appear to reward/punish/complicate values;
- recurring images/objects/phrases;
- contrasting character strategies toward the same underlying problem;
- final state relative to earlier thematic questions;
- writer-authored thematic intent.

The system may propose thematic hypotheses with evidence and counterevidence. It should not assert one correct theme.

### 2.11 The desire to keep reading

This is central and often under-modeled. “Page-turning” is not the same as high action or constant danger.

Contributors can include:

- unanswered but answerable questions;
- anticipated confrontations;
- uncertain outcomes;
- emotional commitments;
- promises awaiting payoff;
- dramatic irony;
- changing goals;
- novelty;
- escalation;
- clear causal momentum;
- compelling voice;
- pleasure of scene execution.

This motivates the explicit `Fount.Intelligence.Reader` model and is explored deeply in `02_EMERGENT_READER_EXPERIENCE_OVER_TIME.md`.

### 2.12 “Meaning and Magic” and irreducible holistic response

The most important lesson from the Nicholl rubric may be that it reserves space for something it does not pretend to formalize completely.

Fount should do the same.

The system can collect evidence associated with distinctiveness, emotional accumulation, thematic resonance, elegant payoff, surprising inevitability, and voice. It should also preserve a human-facing field for **holistic reader response that the model does not reduce**.

That is a feature, not an analytical failure.

## 3. Sundance and the importance of artistic intent

Sundance development materials emphasize fresh cinematic voices, authenticity, singularity, urgency, theme, tone/feel/visual style, intended audience, what the artist wants to work on, why the story matters now, and authentic representation of characters/world.

For Fount, this implies a missing first-class object: **writer intent**.

A project should be able to record explicit authored intent snapshots such as:

- premise/logline;
- creative brief;
- thematic questions;
- intended tone;
- genre expectations to embrace or resist;
- intended audience experience;
- character arc intentions;
- major promises/payoffs;
- deliberate ambiguities;
- things the writer does *not* want “fixed.”

Analysis should compare observed patterns against intent where relevant. Without intent, diagnostics should be phrased more tentatively.

## 4. Craft schools: useful lenses, not the foundation

McKee-style value shifts, Mamet-style scene questions, Johnstone-style status transactions, Hitchcockian suspense, Truby-style moral/character systems, and other frameworks can all provide valuable optional lenses.

The architecture should support them as **named lens packs** with provenance and content hashes. They must not become invisible universal constraints.

Example:

```text
lens pack: mckee_scene_turn@1
  observations: opening_value, closing_value, value_shift

lens pack: johnstone_status@1
  observations: status_bid, status_response, status_reversal

lens pack: suspense_information_gap@1
  observations: audience_knows, character_knows, threat_state, proximity
```

A playbook can request one or more packs. The writer can compare conclusions from different schools.

| theme | hypothesis | across draft | cautious | no |

| “magic” | evidence only | holistic | human-led | **never** |

## 6. Product implications

The system should produce four very different outputs:

1. **Observations:** small source-grounded claims.
2. **State views:** how selected dramatic variables evolve over time.
3. **Diagnoses:** possible explanations of a reported or detected issue.
4. **Revision experiments:** writer-controlled candidate strategies and pages.

A professional-quality product must make those levels visually and structurally distinct.
